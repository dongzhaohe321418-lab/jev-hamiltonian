# Referee report R3 (frozen)

Manuscript: "Is there a Hamiltonian inside a decision model? Probing the energy landscape of Jev for quantum chemistry" (paper/main.tex, compiled paper/main.pdf, 12 pages).
Emphasis of this report: claim calibration, internal consistency and reproducibility.
Assessment boundary: I read the manuscript, bibliography, experiments/PLAN.md, harness/jev.py, all experiment and analysis scripts, the survey notes, and the released JSON. I made no network calls. I reran analyze_e1e3.py, analyze_e4.py, e4_selected_ci.py (in an offline scratch copy with the harness forced to offline) and figures.py; the E1, E3 and E4 outputs, e4_raw.json and all three figure PNGs reproduced byte for byte. I recomputed the E1b, E2, E2b and E3b numbers directly from the released JSON because no script prints most of them. During the review, 459 new cache files and new untracked scripts (e7_collect.py, e8_collect.py, analyze_calib.py) appeared in the working tree, timestamped after the manuscript's data; I did not review them, and every check below uses the 2,235 cache files and the committed experiment files that the manuscript describes.

## Overall assessment

The paper asks whether the per-question probabilities returned by a new multi-head "decision model" (Jev) can be read as one Gibbs landscape, whether Jev can reason about a Hamiltonian written in its input, and whether it is useful for small quantum-chemistry decisions. The engineering is clean. Every API response is cached, the core analyses replay offline and reproduce exactly, the controls chosen after seeing data are mostly labelled exploratory, and the convention-flip control in E2 is a well-designed, discriminating experiment. The negative findings (Jev does not compute with an in-context Hamiltonian; it is dominated by a cheap perturbative estimate for determinant selection) are credible and plainly stated.

The calibration of claims is weaker than the engineering. First, the headline positive claim, that Jev is a useful triage layer "comparable to a two-feature logistic model", does not survive the obvious baseline. The stretch factor is written into every E3 prompt, and on its own it ranks the three labels at least as well as Jev does (AUROC 0.86, 0.83 and 0.93, against Jev's 0.74, 0.85 and 0.89). Second, the quantitative coherence headline (57% of couplings asymmetric beyond three bootstrap standard errors) rests on a criterion that, by my simulation of the authors' own procedure, flags about 12 to 14% of pairs under a perfectly coherent model, and on a noise null whose output ranges from 9% to 32% depending on defensible settings. Third, the described pre-registration deviates from the committed plan in ways the manuscript does not disclose. Fourth, the H2 energy read-out is worse than a constant guess and is limited by a 50 mE_h level spacing, which the text does not mention. The "Hamiltonian" and "energy landscape" framing does real work only in E1; for E2 to E4 it relabels ordinary probability and reasoning questions. None of these points requires new API data, and most can be fixed by reanalysis and rewording, but the abstract and conclusion need to change before the paper's claims match its evidence.

## Who would be interested and why

Readers in cs.LG who evaluate probabilistic outputs of foundation models (coherence, calibration, consistency checks) would find the black-box compatibility protocol of E1 a usable template. It applies to any system that answers several questions about one input, and it adds the odds-ratio symmetry and total-probability identities to the negation and complement checks already used for language models. Computational chemists who are considering fast LLM-style classifiers as routers before expensive calculations would find E3 and E4 useful as a cautionary benchmark, provided the E3 baseline issue below is fixed. The audience is narrowed by the fact that the object of study is one proprietary model version queried on a single day, with no second model for comparison, so the paper's general contribution is the protocol and not the Jev-specific numbers.

## Major strengths

1. Replayable evidence. Every request and response is cached under a content hash, and the E1, E3 and E4 analyses and all three data figures reproduced exactly from the cache in my offline rerun, including the PySCF recomputation of E4.
2. The convention-flip control (E2b). Writing the same Ising energies in the opposite sign convention reverses the rank correlation (n = 4, from -0.41 to +0.34, both verified). This separates "reads coefficient signs" from both "always returns the maximiser" and "knows the energies but orders them wrongly". It is the paper's most convincing single result.
3. Physically grounded labels and proper scoring. E3 uses PySCF labels with stated thresholds, Brier score and ECE alongside AUROC, and reports that Jev's Brier score for RHF instability (0.184) is worse than the base rate (0.177). This honest negative is verified.
4. Candid negative results and limitations. The paper states plainly that Jev is not a calculator, that it loses to first-order perturbation theory in E4, and that its skill collapses without textual cues.
5. The released data contain evidence that supports a key assumption the paper only asserts from a nine-call pilot. For all 48 cases, the five E1 marginals obtained in the 10-question E1b request differ from those in the 5-question E1 request by a median of 0.007 to 0.010 (maximum 0.04). Question-set cross-talk is therefore small at scale, which strengthens the E1 design.

## Major Concerns

R3-M1 [claim-moderation]
**Severity** Major
**Blocking** Yes
**Claim pointer** Jev ranks multireference character, T1 diagnostics and RHF instability with AUROC 0.74 to 0.89, "comparable to a two-feature logistic model", and is therefore useful as a fast triage layer (abstract; Section E3 heading "useful ranking of multireference character"; Discussion paragraph 3; Conclusion).
**Evidence pointer** Table 1; Section 3 "Chemistry ground truth" (each prompt includes "a note stating the stretch factor"); experiments/e1e3_collect.py `state_of` (geometry_note "all bonds stretched to X x equilibrium length"); experiments/qc_labels.json; experiments/e1b_e3b.py `geo_job`.
**Concern** The stretch factor is written into every E3 prompt, and the labels are almost fully determined by it. Positives by stretch 1.0/1.5/2.0/2.5 are 1/6/10/11 (multireference), 2/6/12/10 (T1) and 3/10/12/12 (RHF unstable), out of 12 each. Using the raw stretch number as the score, with no fitting, gives AUROC 0.86, 0.83 and 0.93, which equals or beats Jev (0.74, 0.85, 0.89) on every label. The table's "Logistic (stretch)" row (0.79, 0.76, 0.87) is lower than the raw stretch because leave-one-out refitting adds noise, so the table understates the strongest trivial baseline. Within the one stretch level where labels are mixed (1.5 times equilibrium, n = 12), Jev's AUROC is 0.67, 0.54 and 0.48. Nothing in the evidence shows Jev adds information beyond the number the user typed. Three further problems compound this. (a) No uncertainty is reported for any E3 metric. A molecule-clustered bootstrap gives a 95% interval for Jev's multireference AUROC of 0.60 to 0.91, and the Jev minus two-feature-logistic difference spans -0.26 to +0.05. (b) The Discussion says Jev ranks "multireference character and RHF instability about as well as a small descriptor model", but multireference is Jev's weakest label (0.74, below even the stretch-only logistic at 0.79), and T1 is the only label on which it leads. (c) The abstract says AUROC falls "when the molecule's name is removed", but the geometry-only condition (e1b_e3b.py) removes both the name and the stretch note. The element symbols and coordinates that remain identify most of these molecules (for example "H 0 0 0; H 0 0 0.741"). The experiment therefore cannot attribute the loss to the name, and the likelier explanation is loss of the stretch note.
**Why it matters** The triage claim is the paper's only positive practical conclusion and it appears in the abstract and conclusion. If a constant function of a number supplied by the user matches Jev, the conclusion that Jev "could route molecules between single- and multireference methods" is not supported.
**Resolution test** Add the raw stretch factor (and, if desired, stretch plus gap) as unfitted baselines in Table 1. Report molecule-clustered bootstrap intervals for all AUROC and Brier values and for the Jev minus baseline differences. Report Jev's performance conditional on stretch (for example, stratified AUROC, or a logistic model on stretch with and without Jev's logit, compared by leave-one-molecule-out log loss). Either run name-removed-but-stretch-kept and stretch-removed-but-name-kept conditions, or describe the existing condition accurately as removing both. Revise the abstract, the E3 heading and the Discussion to match.

R3-M2 [statistical-rigor]
**Severity** Major
**Blocking** No
**Claim pointer** "57% of inferred pairwise couplings are asymmetric beyond three bootstrap standard errors" (abstract; Section E1), and 35% of pairs lie beyond the 95th percentile of a coherent null (Section E1, Fig. 2b).
**Evidence pointer** experiments/analyze_e1e3.py (bootstrap over R = 3 repeats; `noise_sd = np.nanmean(np.nanstd(P, 1))`; null with h ~ N(0,1), J ~ N(0,1.5)); Section 3 "Access and energies".
**Concern** A bootstrap over three repeats underestimates the standard error by about a factor of sqrt(2/3) and estimates it very noisily. I simulated the manuscript's exact procedure (three repeats, Gaussian noise, rounding to 0.01, clipping, 300-draw bootstrap over repeats, |z| > 3) on a perfectly coherent two-variable Ising model. It flags 14% of pairs at per-call s.d. 0.013 and 12% at 0.02, against a nominal rate below 1%. The 57% is therefore not a rate of genuine asymmetry. It should be read against a coherent-model false-positive rate of roughly 12 to 14%. The second analysis has the same problem in another form. The per-call noise of 0.013 is a ddof = 0 standard deviation over three values. The ddof = 1 mean is 0.016, the root-mean-square value is 0.020, the 99th percentile is 0.05 and the maximum is 0.13, and the pilot the plan cites gave about 0.028 for the multireference question. The null's field and coupling scales are also arbitrary rather than matched to Jev's observed conditionals. Rerunning that null with a noise s.d. of 0.013, 0.02 and 0.028 moves the fraction of Jev pairs beyond its 95th percentile from 32% to 19% and 9%. Doubling the field scale moves it to about 1%. The qualitative incoherence claim survives on other evidence: the median total-probability residual (0.041) is several times the per-call noise, and two negation pairs miss 1 by more than 0.1 in most calls. The specific numbers in the abstract do not survive in their current form.
**Why it matters** The coherence result is the paper's central contribution to cs.LG, and it is summarised by one number whose meaning depends on an uncalibrated test.
**Resolution test** Calibrate the asymmetry test against a coherent null with the same number of repeats and a matched parameter distribution. The simplest version is a parametric bootstrap from a symmetric joint fitted to each case's observed marginals and conditionals, with a heteroscedastic noise model taken from the observed repeat variance. Report the excess over that null's false-positive rate with an interval, show the sensitivity to the noise s.d., and use ddof = 1. Revise the abstract figure accordingly. Consider stating the total-probability and negation results as the primary evidence for incoherence.

R3-M3 [reproducibility]
**Severity** Major
**Blocking** No
**Claim pointer** The analyses "were written down after a ten-call pilot and before the main data were collected; checks added after seeing data are labelled exploratory. The plan and its amendments are in the repository", and 3 bootstrap SE is "our pre-registered criterion" (Section 3 "Pre-registration"; Section E1).
**Evidence pointer** experiments/PLAN.md (committed 8ee0321 at 22:29:07; amendments at ed07a14); git log; cache timestamps (pilot 22:28:39 to 22:28:45, first main-study call 22:30:21); experiments/e1e3_collect.py; experiments/analyze_e1e3.py.
**Concern** The committed plan differs from what was run and reported in ways the amendment list does not record. (a) E1 statements. The plan lists "open-shell ground state" and "low-lying excited state within 1 eV". The study used "T1 > 0.02" and "RHF unstable", which are also two of the E3 labels. (b) E1 primary criterion. The plan specifies "fraction of pairs whose asymmetry exceeds 3x the noise floor (estimated from repeats)". The paper uses three bootstrap standard errors, which is a different and, per R3-M2, much more liberal test. (c) The plan's positive control ("a set of number statements") is absent. (d) The planned maximum-likelihood inverse temperature for E2 is not reported. (e) E3 planned about 60 cases and has 48. (f) The E4 summary metric (mean log10 error over the curve with a 1e-3 mE_h floor) is not in the plan, which specifies "energy error vs k". Separately, the "ten-call pilot" is nine cached calls, and the plan was committed 74 seconds before the first main-study call, in the same session, to a private repository with no external timestamp. A reader cannot tell from the manuscript that the word "pre-registered" rests on a local commit made one minute before collection.
**Why it matters** The manuscript uses pre-registration to separate confirmatory from exploratory claims and to justify its headline criterion. Undisclosed switches of variables and criterion remove that protection exactly where R3-M2 finds the test to be miscalibrated.
**Resolution test** Add a deviation table (planned versus executed, with reasons and when each change was decided) to the paper or supplement. Report the pre-registered criterion (3 times the repeat noise floor) alongside the executed one. Describe the plan accurately (a time-stamped local commit written immediately before collection), or register future work externally. Relabel as exploratory any analysis whose criterion was changed.

R3-M4 [claim-moderation]
**Severity** Major
**Blocking** No
**Claim pointer** Jev's "energy read-out for H2 is off by 104 mE_h on average" (abstract), its energies "are two orders of magnitude from chemical accuracy" (Discussion), and it varied "by only 25 mE_h across bond lengths where the exact energy varies by 204 mE_h" (E2).
**Evidence pointer** experiments/e2_hamiltonian.py `h2_ask`; experiments/e2_raw.json field `E_read`; experiments/figures.py lines computing `Ej`; Fig. 3c.
**Concern** I reproduced 104, 25 and 204 mE_h using the mapping in figures.py (expected level index times 0.05 E_h). The context changes their meaning. (a) The read-out is a ten-level Score with 50 mE_h spacing, so chemical accuracy (1.6 mE_h) is out of reach by design except through averaging. Comparing it with chemical accuracy measures the instrument as much as the model. (b) A constant guess at the mean exact energy (-1.02 E_h) has a mean absolute error of 65 mE_h. Jev (104 mE_h) is worse than a constant, which the paper does not say. (c) Jev's modal level is the last listed level (-0.75 E_h) in all 24 queries, a pattern consistent with a position or ordering effect that was not tested (see R3-M6). (d) The vendor's own limitations page, as summarised in survey/survey.md, warns that Score levels are "weak in numerical calibration", so the choice of instrument makes the failure partly foreseeable. (e) The released e2_raw.json stores `E_read` values near +1.35 E_h because e2_hamiltonian.py maps the Score as if it were normalised to [0,1] ("checked in E0 output", but E0 contains no Score question). The figure code uses a different, correct mapping. A reader who uses the released `E_read` field gets values that are wrong by more than 2 E_h.
**Why it matters** The 104 mE_h figure is in the abstract and supports the "two orders of magnitude" sentence, but the evidence supports a weaker statement: the read-out is uninformative, worse than a constant, and limited by instrument resolution.
**Resolution test** Report the constant-guess and midpoint baselines. State the level spacing and drop or qualify the comparison with chemical accuracy. Test order sensitivity by reversing the level order. Fix or remove `E_read` in e2_raw.json and correct the comment in e2_hamiltonian.py. Acknowledge the vendor's documented Score limitation.

R3-M5 [writing-clarity]
**Severity** Major
**Blocking** No
**Claim pointer** The title asks whether there is "a Hamiltonian inside a decision model", and the abstract and conclusion frame all four experiments as probing Jev's "energy landscape" and whether it is "useful for quantum chemistry".
**Evidence pointer** Title; abstract; Introduction paragraphs 1 and 4; Sections E2 to E4; Conclusion.
**Concern** Defining E = -log p is a relabelling that holds for any probabilistic model, as the Introduction itself says. The substantive question is whether the elicited conditionals are compatible with a joint distribution. That question is purely probabilistic, and only E1 addresses it. E2 does not probe Jev's landscape. It tests whether Jev can solve a minimisation problem written in its input, which is in-context reasoning, and the phrase "its landscape depends on how the Hamiltonian is written" conflates the answer distribution to one Choice question with a landscape over Jev's own answers. E3 and E4 use only marginals or a single Choice distribution, so no joint landscape is involved. Also, any joint over five binary variables can be written as a Gibbs distribution with higher-order terms, so E1 tests the existence of a joint, not of a pairwise "Hamiltonian-like" one. For a cs.LG reader, the physics vocabulary obscures what was tested: prompted-conditional coherence, in-context arithmetic over an Ising objective, and calibration on chemistry labels.
**Why it matters** The title and abstract promise a physical interpretation that the experiments do not deliver, and an arXiv reader will judge the paper against its title.
**Resolution test** Retitle and reframe around what was measured (for example, coherence of prompted conditionals and in-context Hamiltonian reasoning in a multi-head decision model). Keep the energy language only where it adds content (E1, with "joint distribution" stated plainly). State that E2 tests reasoning about a supplied objective, not a property of Jev's own landscape.

R3-M6 [experimental-design]
**Severity** Major
**Blocking** No
**Claim pointer** Prompt wording "may account for some failures", but "the convention-flip and geometry-only controls separate the two in those experiments" (Limitations).
**Evidence pointer** experiments/e1e3_collect.py (one phrasing per statement, conditioning via "Established fact (computed): ... This statement is TRUE/FALSE."); e4_selected_ci.py (one Choice question); e2_hamiltonian.py (Score levels in ascending order; configuration options always in itertools.product order).
**Concern** Every E1 statement, every conditioning template, the E3 questions, the E4 question and the H2 Score use a single wording and a single option order. Three specific threats follow. (a) E1 conditions by asserting a fact in the input. Half the conditioning facts contradict the PySCF ground truth for that case (for example, "RHF is unstable" for equilibrium H2), and Jev may discount counterfactual facts asymmetrically. That would produce J_ab different from J_ba without any incoherence in a latent joint. The paper treats the prompted response as p(a | b) without testing this. (b) E4 asks for the single most important determinant and then uses the whole Choice distribution as a ranking of all 99 options, which is an extra modelling assumption. (c) The H2 read-out always peaks at the last level (R3-M4). The geometry-only control does not separate wording from knowledge (R3-M1). Only E2b is a clean wording control.
**Why it matters** The paper's broadest conclusion, that Jev's outputs "should not be read as a physical energy landscape", depends on E1 measuring the model and not the conditioning template.
**Resolution test** For E1, use at least two paraphrases per statement and two conditioning templates (for example, "Given that ..." against "Established fact ..."). Report asymmetry separately for conditioning facts that agree and disagree with ground truth. Reverse the option order for the H2 Score and a subset of Ising instances. Report the spread across paraphrases as part of the noise floor.

**Evidence pointer** reproduce.sh; requirements.txt; harness/jev.py; experiments/e2b_convention.py; experiments/e1b_e3b.py; git status.

R3-M8 [experimental-design]
**Severity** Major
**Blocking** No
**Claim pointer** As a selected-CI prior, Jev "beats random selection in all ten systems and loses to a first-order perturbative estimate in nine", and "is dominated by a free perturbative estimate and should not be used" (abstract; Discussion).
**Evidence pointer** experiments/analyze_e4.py (area = mean log10 error with a 1e-3 mE_h floor); e4_selected_ci.py printed "k to 1.6 mEh"; Fig. 4b,c.
**Concern** The counts reproduce (10/10, p = 0.002; 1/10 better than PT1, p = 0.014). They depend on a summary metric that was not planned and that is dominated by the tail of the curve below chemical accuracy, where the 1e-3 floor matters. On the practically relevant metric, the number of determinants needed to reach 1.6 mE_h, the picture is less one-sided. First-order PT needs 79 of 99 determinants for five of the ten systems. Jev reaches the threshold first for N2 at equilibrium (66 against 79) and CH4 (93 against 99), and the two differ by one or two determinants for H2O at 2.0, N2 at 1.5 and C2. The example chosen for Fig. 4b (N2 at equilibrium) shows Jev crossing the chemical-accuracy line before PT1, which the text does not discuss. In a 100-determinant space where the perturbative estimate itself needs most of the space, the experiment has little power to say which ranking is useful for selection.
**Why it matters** The E4 conclusion is directionally plausible, but the strength of "dominated" and "in nine" depends on an unregistered metric choice.
**Resolution test** Report determinants-to-chemical-accuracy per system for every method, the sensitivity of the area metric to the floor, and a note on why Fig. 4b was chosen. Moderate "dominated" if the conclusion depends on the metric. Ideally, add a larger active space where selection matters.
