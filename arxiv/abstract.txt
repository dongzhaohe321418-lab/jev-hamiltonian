Jev is a proprietary "System One" model that answers many typed questions about one input in a single pass and returns a calibrated probability for every allowed answer. Any such output defines local energies E = -log p; we ask whether they form one Hamiltonian-like landscape and whether that landscape is useful for quantum chemistry. Using black-box access to jev-1.13.0, PySCF ground truth and pre-registered analyses, we find that stated facts shift Jev's answers approximately additively in log-odds, so single-fact conditionals predict held-out two-fact conditionals in all 48 cases, yet these effects are not symmetric. Under a coherent null matched to Jev's own noise, 41% of pairwise coupling asymmetries are significant after false-discovery-rate control, the incoherence persists when the exact joint distribution is written into the input, and a symmetric Ising energy predicts no better than an asymmetric dependency network. Given a Hamiltonian as text, Jev follows the signs of the printed coefficients: writing the same Ising energies in the opposite convention reverses its preferences, and for the Jordan-Wigner Hamiltonian of H2 it keeps choosing the textbook Hartree-Fock state after the qubits are relabelled. On quantum-chemistry diagnostics Jev reaches AUROC 0.76-0.89, but most of this comes from a stretch factor stated in the prompt; removing that note drops it to 0.57-0.71. It adds a small amount of information to a two-descriptor model (significant for two of three labels), ranks CCSD(T) failures about as well as the T1 diagnostic, and ranks determinants for selected CI with one template that loses to iterative CIPSI. Two open-weight language models are less coherent still and ignore the Hamiltonian. Jev's probabilities are a fast triage signal whose local energies approximately cohere; they do not form a physical energy landscape.
