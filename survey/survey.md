# Literature survey: a calibrated decision model read as an energy landscape

Bracketed tags are keys in `refs.bib`. Every tagged claim was checked against the source listed for that key in `verification_log.md`. Statements about Jev come only from vendor documentation and press coverage. None of it is peer reviewed, and we treat it as a set of claims to test.

## 0. The object under study: Jev

TypeSafe AI announced Jev on 2026-09-15 and calls it the first "System One model". It is a non-autoregressive model that returns typed, probability-bearing decisions in place of text. The announcement describes the outputs as generated "in a single query", the training as "Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD)", and the model as "calibrated: higher confidence means higher accuracy" [typesafe2026introducing]. TechCrunch gives the training name as "reinforcement learning from calibrated decisions", says the training data is exclusively synthetic, and says the model is transformer-based [fernholz2026new]. The API has three primitives. A *Noul* returns a probability in [0,1]. A *Choice* returns a full distribution over at most 255 options. A *Score* covers 2–10 ordered levels. A request carries one "state" and a map of typed questions, and responses report versioned model ids such as `jev-1.13.0`. No temperature, sampling or logprob controls are documented [typesafe2026api]. The concepts page says questions are asked "independent[ly] ... together" and that the probabilities are "optimized against outcomes to reflect uncertainty" [typesafe2026systemone]. Willison reports that questions "are evaluated in parallel", so sending many questions costs about as much time as sending one [willison2026jev].

Most relevant to us, the vendor's own limitations page says that "many structural invariants one might imagine to hold ... simply aren't guaranteed by the model." It gives an example in which a question and its negation, each asked as a Noul, receive probabilities summing to 1.19. It also warns that Jev "is not a calculator", "does not count reliably", reads dates as text, and has score levels that are "weak in numerical calibration" [typesafe2026jaggedness]. So the vendor itself concedes that the per-question outputs need not be consistent with one another. Whether they are consistent to a degree that can be measured, and in what structured way, is an open empirical question.

## a. Energy-based models, and classifiers read as energy models

An energy-based model assigns a scalar energy to each configuration. Inference clamps the observed variables and minimises energy over the rest, and a Gibbs distribution p ∝ exp(−E) turns energies into probabilities [lecun2006tutorial]. The physics lineage runs through Hopfield's associative memory. It has a Lyapunov energy with Ising-type pairwise couplings, and its stored patterns are local minima [hopfield1982neural]. The Boltzmann machine is the stochastic version, which learns symmetric pairwise weights so that its Gibbs distribution matches the data [ackley1985learning]. Modern (dense) Hopfield networks connect the energy view to transformer attention, and they show that the attention update is the update rule of a continuous Hopfield energy [ramsauer2020hopfield]. Grathwohl et al. read a standard softmax classifier as an EBM. The logits define a joint energy E(x,y) = −f(x)[y], from which the class probabilities and unnormalised p(x) and p(x|y) can all be computed, and energy-based training improves calibration and robustness [grathwohl2019your]. Du and Mordatch scale MCMC-based training of implicit EBMs to high-dimensional image data [du2019implicit]. Winer and Hanin go the other way and treat a randomly initialised MLP as a Hamiltonian over its inputs. They study the Gibbs measure it induces with replica methods [winer2025deep].

*Relevance.* Each Jev Choice or Noul output fixes a local energy E_q(a | s) = −log p_q(a | s) for a question q given the state s. Grathwohl et al. already show how to read a classifier's outputs as energies. The step no prior work takes is to ask whether a *family* of such conditionals, from separate heads queried in parallel on a shared state, composes into one global energy.

## b. Compatible conditionals and joint Gibbs distributions

Whether a set of conditionals has a joint is a classical question. Besag gives the conditions under which local conditional specifications on a lattice define a joint, and he proves the clique-potential (Gibbs) representation of positive Markov fields [besag1974spatial]. That representation is the Hammersley–Clifford theorem, first set out in an unpublished 1971 manuscript [hammersley1971markov]. Arnold and Press characterise when two given conditional families are *compatible*, meaning they come from some joint distribution. The central test is a ratio condition: the ratio of the two conditionals must factor as a product of a function of x and a function of y [arnold1989compatible]. Dependency networks learn each conditional separately and allow cyclic graphs. They are generally inconsistent, and the joint is only defined as the stationary distribution of an ordered Gibbs sampler [heckerman2000dependency]. For binary variables these results give a concrete test. If single-site conditionals come from a pairwise Ising energy, the coupling J_ij inferred from how the log-odds of i change when j is flipped must equal J_ji inferred the other way round. Asymmetry means no pairwise Gibbs joint exists.

A newer line of work tests the coherence of language-model probabilities directly. Fluri et al. find logical inconsistencies in superhuman-level or ground-truth-free settings. Examples are a chess engine that values semantically identical boards differently and GPT-4 forecasts that are non-monotone over time [fluri2023evaluating]. Paleka et al. turn this into an arbitrage-based consistency metric for LLM forecasters. The metric is computed from predictions on logically related questions, for example two mutually exclusive outcomes each given 60% [paleka2025consistency]. Zhu and Griffiths use probabilistic identities and repeated judgments to show that autoregressive LLMs give *incoherent* probability judgments with human-like systematic deviations [zhu2024incoherent]. BeliefBank improves the consistency of a pretrained model's beliefs with a weighted MaxSAT layer and feedback [kassner2021beliefbank]. Calanzone et al. fine-tune with a probabilistic-reasoning loss to improve logical consistency against facts and rules [calanzone2024towards].

*Relevance.* This work tests identities such as negation and complements on text-generating models that verbalise or sample probabilities. None of it tests Arnold–Press compatibility or coupling symmetry on a model that returns native distributions for many questions in parallel over one shared state. Such a model is the case where a dependency-network reading [heckerman2000dependency] applies most directly.

## c. Calibration and proper scoring

Guo et al. show that modern deep networks are miscalibrated and that one temperature parameter fixes much of the problem [guo2017calibration]. Expected calibration error (ECE) comes from Naeini et al.'s Bayesian-binning work [naeini2015obtaining]. The Brier score is the original quadratic score for probability forecasts [brier1950verification]. Gneiting and Raftery give the general theory of strictly proper scoring rules, which includes the log score, the Brier score and CRPS for ordered outcomes [gneiting2007strictly]. For LLMs, Kadavath et al. find that large pretrained models are mostly well calibrated on multiple-choice and true/false formats and can be trained to predict P(IK) [kadavath2022language]. Tian et al. find that RLHF models often give better-calibrated *verbalised* confidences than their token probabilities [tian2023just]. The Paleka et al. benchmark sets proper-scoring accuracy beside consistency [paleka2025consistency].

*Relevance.* Jev's central claim is calibration [typesafe2026introducing, typesafe2026systemone]. Calibration is a property of each marginal against outcomes. It does not imply that the conditionals are jointly coherent: a dependency network can be calibrated head by head and still lack any joint [heckerman2000dependency]. Proper scoring on physics-grounded labels also gives an evaluation that does not depend on the vendor.

## d. Ising/QUBO encodings, annealing, and learning Hamiltonians

Lucas gives Ising (QUBO) formulations of Karp's NP-complete problems and many others. This makes "problem → Hamiltonian → ground state" a standard computational pattern [lucas2014ising]. Quantum annealing in a transverse-field Ising model is the corresponding physical solver [kadowaki1998quantum]. The inverse problem is to recover couplings from samples or conditionals. It is surveyed from pseudo-likelihood and mean-field methods through to data science by Nguyen, Zecchina and Berg [nguyen2017inverse]. Pseudo-likelihood is exactly the Besag conditional construction [besag1974spatial]. On the quantum side, Wiebe et al. introduce Hamiltonian learning and certification with quantum resources [wiebe2014hamiltonian]. Anshu et al. prove sample-efficient learning of local Hamiltonians from Gibbs states [anshu2021sample].

*Relevance.* Fitting J_ij and h_i to Jev's single-site log-odds is an inverse-Ising problem posed on a model's conditionals rather than on samples. Existing Hamiltonian-learning guarantees assume the data come from a real Gibbs state. Jev's conditionals may not be the conditionals of any Gibbs state at all.

## e. Quantum chemistry on quantum and neural hardware

The Jordan–Wigner transformation maps fermionic operators to Pauli strings [jordan1928pauli]. It is the standard route from a molecular Hamiltonian to a qubit Hamiltonian. VQE was first shown on a photonic processor [peruzzo2014variational]. O'Malley et al. computed the H2 dissociation curve on superconducting qubits [omalley2016scalable], which makes H2 in a minimal basis the canonical small test Hamiltonian. The field is reviewed in McArdle et al. [mcardle2020quantum] and Cao et al. [cao2019quantum]. On annealers, Xia, Bian and Kais map electronic-structure Hamiltonians to Ising form, at the cost of an exponential overhead with system size [xia2018electronic]. Genin, Ryabinkin and Izmaylov get around this with a mixed discrete–continuous qubit coupled-cluster method and run it on LiH, H2O and benzene [genin2019quantum]. Neural-network quantum states represent many-body wavefunctions directly [carleo2017solving]. FermiNet [pfau2020ab] and PauliNet [hermann2020deep] reach near-exact ab initio energies for small molecules.

*Relevance.* All of these methods *compute* with the Hamiltonian: they evaluate expectation values, sample from it or optimise over it. None asks whether a general-purpose language-conditioned model can *rank* the spectrum of a qubit Hamiltonian written out in text, for example by ordering computational-basis states of JW-mapped H2 by energy. That question also runs straight into the vendor's stated numeric weaknesses [typesafe2026jaggedness].

## f. Multireference diagnostics, active spaces and selected CI

The T1 diagnostic flags when single-reference coupled cluster is unreliable, with T1 > 0.02 suggesting multireference character [lee1989diagnostic]. Jiang, DeYonker and Wilson examine T1, D1, C0² and %TAE for 3d transition-metal species. They find that no single indicator is reliable and propose joint criteria (T1 > 0.05, D1 > 0.15, |%TAE| > 10) for substantial nondynamical correlation [jiang2012multireference]. Kulik's group trains ML models to predict multireference diagnostics cheaply [duan2020data] and to detect strong correlation during high-throughput transition-metal screening [liu2020rapid]. They also use neural networks to predict spin-state ordering in transition-metal complexes to near-chemical accuracy relative to DFT [janet2017predicting]. For active spaces, AVAS builds them automatically by projecting onto atomic valence orbitals [sayfutyarova2017automated], and Jeong et al. select active spaces with ML trained on bond dissociation [jeong2020automation]. Selected CI goes back to CIPSI [huron1973iterative]. Heat-bath CI makes selection efficient with a coefficient-times-matrix-element threshold [holmes2016heat]. Coe trains a neural network on the fly to predict which determinants are important [coe2018machine].

*Relevance.* Each of these steps is a typed decision that fits Jev's schema. Is the system multireference (Noul)? Which spin state is the ground state (Choice)? How much static correlation is there (Score)? Which determinants should be kept (Choice or Noul over candidates)? Every prior ML model here is a task-specific model trained on computed descriptors. None tests a general, text-conditioned calibrated decision model against these labels, and none checks whether its confidences follow the physics labels (for example, T1 or ΔE_spin) rather than surface features of the molecule's name or formula.

## g. LLMs and foundation models in chemistry

ChemCrow gives GPT-4 18 expert-designed chemistry tools [bran2024augmenting]. Coscientist plans and runs experiments autonomously [boiko2023autonomous]. ChemBench compares LLMs with expert chemists on more than 2,700 question–answer pairs. It finds that the best models, on average, beat the best human chemists in the study, yet struggle with some basic tasks and give overconfident predictions [mirza2025framework]. More generally, LLM mathematical reasoning is fragile when numbers or clauses are perturbed [mirzadeh2024gsm]. Jev's documentation says outright that it is not a calculator [typesafe2026jaggedness]. These systems are generative and tool-using. Jev is a non-generative decision layer that could sit inside such an agent, for instance as a router or triage step [fernholz2026new, willison2026jev]. No study has measured whether such a layer's confidences are safe to use in a scientific workflow.

## Summary of positioning

The EBM view [lecun2006tutorial, grathwohl2019your] says Jev's outputs *can* be read as energies. Compatibility theory [besag1974spatial, arnold1989compatible, heckerman2000dependency] says when those energies form one landscape. LLM-coherence work [fluri2023evaluating, paleka2025consistency, zhu2024incoherent] shows that generative LLMs often fail simpler identities. The vendor admits similar failures for Jev [typesafe2026jaggedness]. Our experiments put these together. We test coupling symmetry and cycle consistency on a native-distribution, parallel-query model. We test whether the model can rank the spectra of real qubit Hamiltonians [jordan1928pauli, omalley2016scalable]. We score calibrated usefulness on standard quantum-chemistry decisions [lee1989diagnostic, janet2017predicting, holmes2016heat] with proper scoring rules [gneiting2007strictly].
